Using adjusted income means for example, if the price inflation was 10% from 2012 to 2018, that a person earning 500’ in 2012 and 550’ in 2018 would be able to purchase the same amount of goods in the two years, so the income in real terms is the same. I.e. we measure purchasing power instead of the nominal income, which is really not directly comparable.

We use the suggested code to get

with(cardata, tapply(income_med_adj, year, mean))
##     2012     2018 
## 652.3436 656.9573

So, the real income is more or less the same.

We see, particularly in 2018 that almost all municipalities are close to major cities, the exeption being Averøy, close to Kristiansund. Regarding the incentives, people living in municipalities close to larger cities very often have work in the city. Going by an ordinary car means rush hour traffic and usually toll roads to get to work. With an el-car, they can use public lanes to avoid queueing, and also avoid toll fares. (One could additionally see that many of these municipalities are high-income, so families can easily afford two cars, which makes the el-car a great second alternative.) For Averøy, we need some local knowledge. Here a tunnel leading to Kristiansund was charging substantial road toll fares, and many people in Averøy have their jobs in Kristiansund, so it would pay off to purchase an electric car to avoid the toll fares. Here the traffic level is low, and there are no “public lane incentive” working here.

We see a large variability in diesel percentages, and the clear dependency on sentrality. On the other hand, there has been discussions on banning diesel cars in city centers and so on, making gasoline and el-cars more favorable choices there.

OK, are el-cars really replacing conventional cars? This is not easy to answer completely in a reliable way, however, we can simply see if the number of conventional cars per capita goes down from 2012 to 2018. Otherwise, the electric cars comes on top of an increasing mass of other cars. Let’s see.

#compute total number of diesel and gasoline cars by year
C_cars <- with(cardata, tapply(diesel + gasoline , year, sum))
#compute total population by year
Tot_pop <- with(cardata, tapply(population, year, sum))
#show the counts, and then divide to get per capita counts.
C_cars
##    2012    2018 
## 2403401 2323637
Tot_pop
##    2012    2018 
## 4985870 5295619
C_cars/Tot_pop
##      2012      2018 
## 0.4820425 0.4387848

So, the per capita number of conventional cars is slightly lower in 2018, which is likely to imply that electric vehicles has substituted some of the conventional car park. We can have a look at the total number of cars per capita as well:

#compute cars by year in total
Tot_cars <- with(cardata, tapply(total, year, sum))
#divide to get per capita cars in total
Tot_cars / Tot_pop
##      2012      2018 
## 0.4877355 0.5133500

So, there are more cars per capita in 2018.
